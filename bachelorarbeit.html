Thesis title

Bachelor's thesis · 2020

Dynamic Simulation of the Cracking Process in Connecting Rod Manufacturing, Considering Crack Propagation

Automotive connecting rods are manufactured by fracture splitting because it creates a highly precise, unique joint between rod and cap, enabling accurate mounting on the crankshaft without additional machining.

Situation

The most highly loaded part in the engine

The connecting rod links piston and crankshaft: it turns the up-and-down motion of the piston into rotation and transmits the entire energy of combustion. To mount it on the crankshaft, its big end has to be split into two halves – rod and cap – which are bolted back together around the crankshaft.

Complication

Breaking it on purpose

However, this simple and effective manufacturing process poses challenges for manufacturing simulation when it comes to comparing different variants, solely based on simulation, in terms of their form stability.

  • LighterTo reduce oscillating masses, many geometry variants with less material are being tested.
  • StrongerRising engine power and firing pressures call for new high-strength steels.
  • Still round?After cracking and bolting, the deformation of the big end can exceed what honing can correct.

Approach

The whole crack station in one dynamic model

A dynamic finite element simulation reproduces the cracking process – from the impact of the wedge to the crack running through the big end. Cracking and the subsequent bolting are simulated in one model, because their deformations can compensate each other.

Why it matters

Lighter con rods without surprises: geometry variants can be checked virtually for form stability before tools and prototypes are built – and the method can be applied to different con rod models.
