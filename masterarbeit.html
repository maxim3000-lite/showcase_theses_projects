Thesis title

Master's thesis · 2022

Validation of a New Method to Compute the Gearing Tooth Root Failure

How much force can a hardened steering pinion take before a tooth breaks – and can we predict it without simulating the entire heat treatment?

Situation

Every steering move passes through a few teeth

The steering gear turns the rotation of the steering wheel into a linear movement of the rack, which moves the wheels. In a rack-and-pinion gear, the complete steering force is transmitted through the tooth contact between pinion and rack.

Complication

Hardened – but how strong exactly?

The pinion is induction-hardened: its surface layer turns into hard martensite. Two effects make the tooth stronger – the harder surface and compressive residual stresses from the volume expansion of the hardened layer.

  • Misuse load casePushing the car off a curb by steering at standstill – the driver applies the load without any limiter.
  • Costly to simulateFull process-chain simulations of the heat treatment need extensive lab data, time and money.
  • Hard to accessProduction is partly outsourced – samples and process data are hard to get.

Research question

research_question.txt

How can the static strength of an induction-hardened pinion be predicted reliably – including hardening contour and residual stresses – with a fast, cheap and universally applicable method?

Approach

The shortcut: build the hardened state straight into the model

Instead of simulating the entire heat treatment, the FE model is generated synthetically – with a realistic hardness distribution and the matching residual stresses, but without computing the microstructural transformation behind them.

Why it matters

The pinion is a safety-relevant part. The aim: a validated shortcut that brings the design for reliability according to the FKM nonlinear guideline into early development – at a fraction of the effort of a full process-chain simulation.
